Precedents

History teaches us more about the future than any extrapolation can

The Mainframe

In 1970, sharing a computer made sense. The machine was expensive, it needed specialist staff, and most users needed only a fraction of its capacity. A university or a company could own one computer and divide its time among many people.

The early personal computer looked like a poor substitute. It had far less computing power and memory, and much less software. If the question was how to replace a mainframe, buying one made little sense.

IBM had built its business selling computers to organizations. It introduced its mass-market PC in 1981, after Apple and other early manufacturers had begun selling their machines. IBM’s entry helped turn what many still regarded as a hobbyist’s toy into a business tool.22

A machine on your desk gave you all of its limited capacity, whenever you wanted it. You could spend an evening learning to program, revising a budget, or trying something that might not work, without paying for each hour of computer time.

Useful software made that freedom worth buying. In 1979, VisiCalc gave people a reason to purchase an Apple II just to run a spreadsheet. They could change an assumption in a budget, watch the totals recalculate, and try another. A computer became something a business owner could use directly in the course of an ordinary working day.22

Steve Jobs with the Macintosh, January 1984. Photo: Bernard Gotfryd, public domain.

People bought these machines while they were still weak by the standards of institutional computing. Being powerful enough for the owner’s work, and always available to do it, mattered more than matching the largest computer on the market. Mainframes continued serving large organizations while a new industry grew around people buying machines for themselves.

The Best Businesses Sell a Product They Don’t Own

, the world’s largest taxi company, owns no vehicles.Tom Goodwin, TechCrunch, 201523

Airbnb followed the same logic with accommodation. Drivers supply cars; hosts supply homes. These companies make that capacity easy to find, book and pay for. They can add supply by bringing in another driver or host, without buying the car or building the house themselves.

With personal AI, the user can supply both the computer and its electricity. The company supplies the agent: the software that understands what the user wants, works with their apps, and makes the machine useful.

An AI company that rents its GPUs still has to pay for more computation as its users do more with their agents. Running those agents on the users’ own machines changes who pays for that capacity.

We think the best AI companies will provide inference without owning or renting the GPUs.

Apple and Nvidia already show part of this model. Nvidia’s DGX Spark, which began shipping in October 2025, puts an AI computer on the customer’s desk.24 Apple sells devices that can run its models locally. The customer buys the hardware and supplies the electricity.

Apple’s approach also leaves room for the cloud. Tasks that need more computation can go to Private Cloud Compute. The point is to let the customer’s machine do the work it can, and use remote capacity where it earns its cost.

Hybrid Inference

We do not think everything moves local. We think most of it does, and that the cloud stays for the moments that need it, at a fraction of its current share. That is already how computing works, and nobody finds it strange.

Think about where the work happens over a day on a MacBook. Moving the mouse, scrolling a page, writing a document: all of it runs on the machine in front of you, and once you own the machine it costs nothing more. A few times an hour you reach past it. You type something into Google, and for a fraction of a second a datacenter throws thousands of machines at your question. You open a website, and a server sends you the page. You hit send, and a mail server carries the message. Then the work comes back to your desk. Nobody calls that a cloud computer or a local computer. It is both, in a proportion you never have to think about.

The chart draws that day as one line. The dashed line across the middle is the on-device threshold. Below it, the work can be done on your own machine. Above it, the work needs a server somewhere else. Most of the day sits below the line. The 3 spikes are the moments you reached for the cloud, and each one lasted a fraction of a second. Hover over a spike, or tap it, and the chart shades the part above the line and marks it with a dollar sign. That is the part somebody has to pay for, every time it happens. Everything below the line is already paid for.

Now draw the same day for AI as it is served today. Every request goes to a frontier model in a datacenter, so the whole line sits above the threshold. Reading your email, ordering lunch, drafting a reply: all of it runs in the cloud, and all of it is billed by the token. The hard problems are up there too, but they are a small part of the day. That is why we think tokens are being needlessly wasted. Not because the cloud is used, but because it is used for everything.

There is a better way, because not every moment needs a frontier model. Reading your email, writing a reply, cross-pollinating your contacts, remembering what you said last week: a local model does these as well as a frontier model does, on your own GPU, at no marginal cost. Switch the chart above to hybrid inference and the line drops below the threshold for the whole day. It crosses the line twice, once to analyze a blood report and once to solve a hard problem. Those are the moments a frontier model earns its price.

Inference settles into the shape computing already has. The agent that runs all day, reading your screen and your mail, runs on your own GPU, because that is where the context is and because the marginal token is free. The hard problem, the one that needs a frontier model and a minute of thought, crosses the line for a few seconds and comes back. The cloud does not disappear. Its share just drops from everything to a few spikes a day.

The Innovator’s Dilemma

If you were choosing a company to build a personal AI computer, Apple would be the obvious choice. It designs the chips, controls the operating system, and knows how to turn a complicated technology into something people want in their homes.

The difficulty begins with the App Store.

Apple has spent years helping developers build businesses around software. Making those apps more useful with AI fits comfortably into that business. Letting AI replace some of them is a more awkward proposition.

Consider something small: an app you bought to organize a project or keep track of expenses. You probably wanted a fraction of its features and had to learn where someone else decided to put them. If your agent can make the tool you actually need, there may be no reason to shop for an app at all. You describe what you want, try it, and ask for changes.

Other software will still be worth buying. Services will still need customers. But increasingly, your agent could choose and operate that software for you. The companies behind it would have to earn their place by being useful to your agent, with fewer opportunities to keep you inside their own interfaces.

This is the same trap Google fell into when deciding whether to launch a chatbot. Doing so would result in the cannibalization of its search business, which ended up happening anyway as it had to compete with ChatGPT.

Apple may decide that making a better computer is worth changing how software is sold. It has reinvented its products before. But it has an enormous existing business to bring through that transition.

We can start with the assumption that the agent will operate the computer. It should be able to use existing apps, work across them, and make its own tools where that serves you better.

But doing all of this takes a startup that is unafraid to push the boundaries and can also build a supply chain, create AI-first silicon, write an operating system meant for an agent, and rebuild the computing experience around intelligence.

We spent two years doing that, and we think thousands of others will follow suit in the next 5.
