The Three Caps

Not all things are built at the speed of bits

The Grid

A datacenter is, before anything else, a connection to the electric grid. Google now calls transmission the single biggest threat to its datacenter expansion, and in parts of the country the wait for a connection has passed ten years.2 A hundred-megawatt connection averages seven years, and Berkeley Lab data show that the wait has doubled.3

These delays are not administrative accidents. They are the physical schedule of high-voltage construction: routing, permitting, transformer backlogs, eminent domain. Chips come out of a fab in months. A transmission line takes years, which is how you end up with GPUs sitting in warehouses, waiting for electricity that does not exist yet.

The Neighbors

Suppose the grid were solved. A datacenter is still a windowless box the size of several football fields that hums through the night and draws on the local water and power. People do not want one next door, and they have started to say so in ways that cost money. Data Center Watch counted $64B of American projects blocked or delayed by local opposition.4 By early 2026 the count had reached roughly $130B in a single quarter, with 833 opposition groups organized across 49 states, and the second quarter added forty-five more projects worth $68B.5

And the opposition is not a fringe. In the poll above, sixty-nine percent of voters oppose a datacenter in their own community, a majority of Democrats, Republicans, and independents alike, and a separate Annenberg survey puts the figure at three in five.6

The State

Where the neighbors organize, the state eventually follows. In August 2026 Governor Greg Abbott halted datacenter grid approvals across Texas pending an audit,7 an order that froze four hundred and seventy-four gigawatts of pending connection requests, more than five times the record peak demand of the entire ERCOT system.8 In September he extended the halt to every environmental permit a datacenter needs, and the audit is due in December. New York went further. Governor Hochul froze new hyperscale datacenters by executive order in July, the legislature passed a one-year ban behind it, and nearly two-thirds of New Yorkers polled want the ban kept when it expires.9

Nor is this a passing mood in two statehouses. Wood Mackenzie counts more than four hundred datacenter moratoriums enacted since January across forty-two states, touching nearly a fifth of the sites it tracks and more than a hundred gigawatts of planned capacity, and the Texas pause alone puts sixty gigawatts of projects under audit.10

Then came the midterms. Going in, NBC found sixty-four percent of voters less likely to support a candidate who backed a local datacenter, and eleven percent more likely. On November 3 datacenters were on the ballot forty-one times in ten states, and the industry won once, in Harwood, North Dakota. Wells, Maine passed a retroactive six-month moratorium with more than eighty percent of the vote. Of the seventy-four candidates for governor that Data Center Watch tracked, forty-three percent had backed a moratorium or a ban, and that side won the statehouses that matter most. Texas, which holds about a quarter of the datacenter capacity in the works, went to Gina Hinojosa on a promise to freeze approvals until the legislature writes rules; Ohio went to Amy Acton and Georgia to Keisha Lance Bottoms, who ran on the same issue. In the NBC exit poll, fifty-six percent of voters called AI and datacenters a major factor in their vote for Congress.10 The political price of saying yes to a datacenter is no longer rising. It has been paid, in public, by candidates who said yes, and nothing about the demand curve in Figure 1 will bring it back down.

So what’s the reality?

Against those hundred gigawatts, what is actually being built? Goldman Sachs projects American datacenter additions of 13.6 gigawatts in 2026 and 36.3 in 2027, the most optimistic reputable schedule in print.11 But a schedule is not a building. Jefferies observes that construction has begun on only twelve of the twenty-four gigawatts planned for 2026, that as much as eighty percent of the 2027–28 pipeline has not broken ground, and that fifteen to twenty gigawatts a year is the realistic pace.12

All of it is borrowed, and rates are rising

Everything above rests on one more assumption, and it no longer holds: cheap money. Datacenters are built with borrowed money, and this buildout has borrowed more than $300B of it, much of that as floating-rate private credit, which means the interest bill moves with the Fed. The Fed is once again raising rates into it.13 Every unfinished building in the queue now costs more to carry each month than it did when the loan was signed, and the lenders have noticed. CoreWeave, the largest of the so-called neoclouds, has watched its bonds trade past sixteen percent yields, and credit default swaps imply roughly sixty-three percent odds of default within five years.14 The week of the midterms the spread passed 1,100 basis points, and on the morning after the vote CoreWeave fell ten percent while Microsoft and Amazon each rose six, because capacity that already exists is suddenly worth more than capacity that still needs a permit. Gartner expects as many as eighty percent of neoclouds to cease independent operations by 2030. The Dallas Fed and the Bank for International Settlements have each warned that private credit is mispricing the sector’s risk.15

None of this requires a crash to matter. It requires only that the marginal datacenter, the one at the end of the queue, financed at the top of the rate cycle and opposed at the county meeting, does not get built.

That is all it takes. Put an exponential demand curve against a supply curve flattened by energy, land, and politics, and the gap between them is a trillion-dollar opening for AI that runs locally, on a machine of your own.

Modeled out, that gap comes to $5T of displaced cloud demand by 2030.16 Served locally, it is five hundred and fifty million personal AI computers that someone has to build.

So there are three candidates for how AI actually reaches a billion people:

  1. Just build more, bro
  2. Put them into space
  3. A GPU per person

We think history is repeating itself, and that the simplest of the three is the one that catches the unmet demand. The next part takes each in turn.
