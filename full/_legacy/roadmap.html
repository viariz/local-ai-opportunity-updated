Next-Gen Chips

The Von Neumann Bottleneck

Every processor in a datacenter today descends from a machine built to do exact arithmetic. That was the right design for seventy years, because a payroll has to come out to the cent. A language model does not work that way. It models probabilities and samples from them, and most of its arithmetic tolerates far less precision than the hardware insists on providing. So a great deal of the energy in an AI datacenter goes into a numerical exactness that never shows up in the answer.

Once you see that, a different question opens up: how much of the work can the physics of the chip itself do? There are three serious answers.

Thermodynamic chips use the noise in a circuit to sample probabilities directly. It is the most radical of the three and the furthest from production. Photonics does the matrix multiplications with light, and compute-in-memory does them inside the memory array where the model’s weights are already stored. Both of those keep the arithmetic ordinary and change where it happens. We are betting on compute-in-memory, and the reason is easiest to see by following the data.

The von Neumann bottleneck

A processor is only as fast as the memory that feeds it, and conventional computers keep the two apart. Every time a model runs, its weights are hauled across the gap between memory and processor, billions of parameters, over and over, for every token. The hauling is where the time and the energy go. That is the von Neumann bottleneck, and it is why a chip’s headline speed says so little about how well it runs a model.

Compute-in-memory does the key operations where the weights already live, so most of that traffic never happens. It does not make the processor faster. It removes the trip. That is the architecture Truffle is built around, and it is why the chips in this chapter are not GPUs.

The T1

The chip in the first machines comes out of a partnership with a compute-in-memory designer we are not naming yet. A computer company lives or dies by its chip relationship, so we have put real work into this one. It is in good shape, and the chip ships under Truffle’s own name: the T1.

Under the name, the T1 is the TM50, the compute-in-memory part our partner brought up this year. The specs:

Compute320 TOPS @ INT8 · 200 TFLOPS @ bFP16
Memory96 GB, with 128 GB unified
Memory bandwidth307.2 GB/s
Typical power~20 W
Runs locally180B Qwen Next or equivalent MoEs

At our behest, one of our board members has also invested in our silicon partner directly. We sit on both sides of the table, on their cap table and in their order book, and that is leverage we intend to keep using.

The T2

After the first ten thousand units, by the end of 2027, we have a verbal commitment to license our partner’s technology for $10M and build its successor ourselves: the T2, a multi-chip package manufactured by Truffle in South Korea. That is the point where the chip, like everything else in the machine, becomes ours.

The T2’s silicon is the TM60, a custom version of our partner’s design, shaped by what we learn from the first ten thousand units in the field. It will have no reliance on Chinese manufacturing or Chinese connections: a purely Western build.

We are doing this for two reasons:

  1. To reduce our exposure if the US–China relationship sours.
  2. To get as close to full vertical integration as we can.

The numbers:

Silicontwo T2s, tensor parallel
Compute600 TOPS @ INT8 · ~384 TFLOPS @ bFP16
Memory bandwidth600 GB/s
Powerunder 30 W
First samplesQ2 2027

About double the T1 in compute and in memory bandwidth, at close to the same power.

The Supply Chain

A chip is not a computer. Around the T1 sit the casting, the injection molding, the finishes, the thermals, the boards, the compliance work, and the people who do each of those, and two years ago none of them existed for us. We spent that time learning how hard our problem was. Solving it meant building a network of relationships, so we built one.

We went to China and searched for chip vendors, computer manufacturers, and everyone in between: casting, injection molding, CMF, and the rest. We now have working relationships with every vendor we need and a direct path to a million computers in the next five years.

Doing this properly means owning the whole chain. So next year’s goal is not only to sell ten thousand computers. It is also to build a chip packaging facility in South Korea that packages our dies from TSMC, so that the chain from wafer to desk runs through Truffle. Apple went the same way when it moved the Mac onto its own silicon.

The Timeline

We learned from the best. It took Apple decades to conclude that it needed its own silicon; we started from that conclusion, and a year ago we put the company on a path to custom silicon designed for an inference computer.

That early call on AI compute is going to pay out for years.
