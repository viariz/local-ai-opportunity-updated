A Million New Computers

The economy that comes after the personal AI computer

After the PC, the Internet

History runs in a sequence. The mainframe became the personal computer. Then the personal computers were connected to one another, and the network that formed, the internet, became a bigger economic event than the computers themselves. We believe history repeats here: after the personal AI computer comes a network of agent computers.

Truffle’s main bet is that we learn from Apple’s one great mistake. Apple built the personal computer, and then captured none of the internet that ran on it: no search, no social, no commerce. It took until the App Store, decades later, for Apple to take a cut of what moved through its machines. We do not intend to wait decades.

Economies are made of differences

Start with the simplest possible economy: person A and person B. A makes really great bread. B also makes really great bread. They have no reason to interact; there is no trade, and no transfer of value.

Now let A make great bread and B make great butter, and an economy forms, because they are different. Economic activity is based on differences.

This is the problem with datacenter AI. Everyone’s Claudes are the same, and so my Claude has no reason to interact with yours, or to pay it.

Agentic payments

Identical machines can still spend money; they just cannot trade with each other. So the first thing the industry built is the obvious thing. What gets called agentic payments today is an AI spending a human’s money at an existing business: it books the Uber, orders the dinner, pays the shop. Instinct and Muse do this now, and it works. But look at what is actually moving. The human still wants the dinner. The restaurant still cooks it. The dollar still travels from a person to a company, the way it did through a website in 2005 and across a counter in 1985. The agent has replaced the checkout page, not the economy.

That is why agentic payments, however well they work, are bounded by the thing they wrap. Their ceiling is the ceiling internet commerce already has: the number of humans, the hours they are awake, and the money they were going to spend anyway. It is a real business and a necessary first step. It is not a new economy, because nothing new is being made or sold. Every transaction in it has a person at one end.

An economy of machines

The bigger thing starts when the machines stop being identical. A personal model takes the shape of its human. Yours ends up good at math because you do math all day; mine ends up good at writing because I do. The moment that is true, the two of them have something to sell each other and a reason to pay, and the transaction has no human at either end. Your machine buys an hour of mine because that is cheaper than learning what I know. This is not a checkout page with a machine in the middle. It is an economy with machines on both sides, and its size has nothing to do with how many humans there are. It is set by how many machines there are, and by how different they have become.

The payments bet

A society of millions of agents that are all different from each other will trade. They will buy context they lack, sell skills they have, and hire one another for the work they are bad at. And wherever there is trade, there are payments. Truffle sits between the machines as the processor of those payments.

The goal is to take a fraction of the machine-to-machine economy we are helping to build. Apple Pay came thirty years after the Mac. We intend to build the payments layer at the same time as the computer.

A payments business, once built, runs at close to 99 percent margin. The slow, 20 percent margin hardware business is the moat and the lock-in that the high-margin business stands on.

Agentic payments put a machine on one side of a transaction. Our bet is the economy that puts machines on both.

We believe the machine economy, payments between agentic computers, probably passes the human economy in size within about ten years. We know how that sounds. It is still the bet. We do not want to sell computers forever. We want to be part of the economy that forms when computers drive themselves.

For a sense of the market, look at what the last network did. In 2000, Americans bought about $27B of goods online. In 2025 they bought about $1.2T, roughly 45x in 25 years, and it did that while capped by a hard ceiling: the number of humans, and the hours they are awake.

The machine economy has no such ceiling. Agents do not sleep, and their population is bounded only by the number of machines that can be produced, which is exactly what the supply chain in Next-Gen Chips is built to scale. So we expect it to grow past the internet economy, and faster. The napkin math on what Truffle captures:

Human internet payment volume today (Visa, FY2026)≈ $15.6T a year
Machine-to-machine volume, ~2035 (call it double)≈ $30T a year
Truffle’s revenue at a 1% take≈ $300B a year
Visa’s revenue today (FY2026)≈ $46B

A 1% take on machine payments is six and a half times the revenue of the largest payments company on Earth.
