The Local AI Opportunity

Why AI will diffuse through society as a GPU per person

@iamgingertrash

Every era of computing has had its own way of running the meter. On a mainframe, you bought time: a few minutes on an expensive machine that belonged to somebody else. The internet made us familiar with bandwidth. We learned to think of a connection as a pipe, and paid for a wider one when we wanted more to flow through it. Now we are learning to count tokens, the little pieces of language a model reads and produces as it works.

As tokens get cheaper, we can afford to give the machine jobs that would once have seemed extravagant. Something similar happened when Britain learned to get more work out of a ton of coal. A more efficient steam engine saved its owner fuel, but it also made steam power affordable to the next factory owner, who had previously done without. Multiply that decision across an industrializing country and the coal saved by individual engines was overtaken by the coal burned in new ones. William Stanley Jevons described the result in 1865:

“It is wholly a confusion of ideas to suppose that the economical use of fuel is equivalent to a diminished consumption. The very contrary is the truth.”W. Stanley Jevons, The Coal Question, 1865

Since ChatGPT arrived, we have kept spending the savings on more ambitious requests. A question that once seemed worth a short answer becomes worth an afternoon of research; a useful coding suggestion makes us wonder whether the model could build the whole thing. As these jobs become affordable and reliable, they enter the routine, and our idea of normal use expands to include them.

2023 · Chatbots

The first chatbots answered simple questions:

What is the capital of France?
Paris is the capital of France.
How many ounces are in a pound?
There are 16 ounces in a pound.

Each exchange cost a few dozen tokens, and a whole day of use rarely crossed 100.

2025 · Early agents

Then came short-horizon agents that could do small jobs on their own, like answering email, browsing the web, or running a piece of research for you:

Who are the 5 biggest home-battery makers, and how do their prices compare?
web.search(“biggest home battery manufacturers”) browser.read(14 pages) docs.extract(spec_sheets: Tesla, BYD, LG, Enphase, Sonnen) calc.normalize(“$ / kWh installed”) → Powerwall 3 ≈ $1,000/kWh; BYD undercuts it at… truncated

One of these chats ran to 5,000 tokens or more, and nobody minded, because the work was worth it.

2026–27 · Strong agents

Now there are strong agents that carry a long task from start to finish, building a piece of software end to end or reverse-engineering an app, in runs that last tens of hours:

Port our billing service off the legacy API and migrate the tests.
repo.read(212 files) code.map(“legacy_api.*”) → 47 call sites code.rewrite(“billing/client.ts”) tests.run() → 14 failures tests.run() → pass webhooks.migrate() — day 2… truncated

A run like that burns 100,000 tokens without trying, and the number climbs with every improvement in memory, context, and tool use, because each one makes a longer run worth paying for.

2027½ and beyond · Always-on

The last step is the loop that never closes: an agent that is never prompted and never stops.

New information is just another injection into its stream of tokens, and it runs all day and all night on behalf of 1 human. The first of these are already here, in Grok Bot, Instinct, Muse, and Dot. The curve goes vertical when they become ordinary.

We keep finding more work for AI, but supplying the tokens is becoming the harder problem. A more capable agent gives millions of people a reason to ask more of it, and that extra demand can arrive with a software update.

Those extra tokens, however, still have to come from machines running in datacenters.

Expanding those facilities means securing electricity, land, and permission to build, which can take years. The chart shows the opportunity created when demand for tokens pulls sharply away from the cloud’s capacity to supply them.1

Even if energy, land, and regulation were no obstacle, there would still be no obvious ceiling on the work we could find for intelligence. This creates a situation I described to some of my investors in May:

The Agentic Paradox

The better an agent becomes, the longer it is worth keeping it at work. Better memory and tool use can increase its consumption even without making the model larger. If the model also requires more computation per token, we are asking each machine to do more work for each user while every user wants more of its time.

We think much of that unmet demand will eventually find its way onto a personal inference machine. A computer you buy and keep running, producing tokens throughout the day, with electricity as its main ongoing cost.

It is an incredibly unsexy idea at a time when sexy ideas command enormous premiums. But who really wants a computer you plug into the wall when you can have mass drivers on the Moon, or cover the Sahara with “intelligence factories,” the modern Mecca of AI psychosis victims?

A remarkably similar thing happened in the 1970s. Much of computing revolved around large machines owned by institutions, with users buying or being allocated time on them. The first personal computers looked unimpressive beside those installations. They had little memory, limited software, and owners who often had to work quite hard to make them useful.

But as the machines improved, someone who wanted to write a document or keep the accounts for a small business could increasingly do it on a computer of their own. Each useful new application gave another person a reason to buy one. Computing began spreading through purchases made by its users, into buildings that already existed, drawing on electricity already available there.

Suppose 1 billion people each have a personal agent that reads their messages, follows their work on screen, and uses their apps to get things done. For this calculation, assume each agent generates 50 tokens a second, continuously. Serving that workload from datacenters would require:

Tok/s required5 × 1010 tokens per second
Tokens per day≈ 4.3 quadrillion
H200 equivalents≈ 80,000,000 GPUs
Power at roughly 2 joules per token100 GW, continuous
Energy per year≈ 880 TWh
Cost to build≈ $3.5 trillion

100 gigawatts is roughly the generating capacity of the entire American nuclear fleet. Under our assumptions, personal agents would keep the equivalent of that fleet occupied around the clock, before accounting for model training or other uses of AI.

The natural response is to build more datacenters. I expect we will build a great many. What I doubt is that they will arrive fast enough to keep up with what we want from them. Approving a construction budget does not settle when the electricity will be available, or whether the community chosen to host the project wants it there. Those decisions belong to utility planners, neighbors, and elected officials, none of whom is obliged to share an AI company’s timetable.
